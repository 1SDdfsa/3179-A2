Follow one grain on its journey: from the paddock, through the rain that grows it and the harvest, to a port, and on to a kitchen overseas.

"Grain" here means three families of crops: cereals such as wheat and barley, oilseeds such as canola, and pulses, the dried seeds of peas and beans such as lentils and chickpeas.

1What counts as a grain

Australia is forecast to harvest about 64 million tonnes of grain in 2026–27.

It is mostly cereals, and wheat alone is nearly half of everything. Then comes canola, then pulses like lentils.

2Where it grows

Wheat grows in a crescent across the south: the Western Australian wheatbelt, South Australia, northern and western Victoria, and inland New South Wales up to Moree.

Western Australia grows the most winter grain. Queensland's crop is small this year: 2.2 million tonnes, down from a record 3.9 million last year.

3Why there: the rain

Wheat is sown in autumn and harvested in early summer, so it needs rain from April to October. In the southern grain belt that is exactly when the rain comes: about two-thirds of the year's rain at Merredin, Minnipa and Horsham. Moree, further north, gets most of its rain in summer, so its wheat leans partly on summer rain stored in the soil.

4The grain year

Wheat is harvested from October to January. Then it goes to the ports.

Ships load most from December to May, after harvest: those six months carry 57% of an average year's wheat exports. October and November are quietest, just before the new harvest reaches the ports.

5Boom and bust

The size of the harvest swings hugely with the weather. The biggest winter crop (2022–23, 69.4 million tonnes) was nearly five times the smallest (1994–95, 14.7 million tonnes), a drought year. The 2026–27 forecast, 60.9 million tonnes, would be the fourth largest on record.

6What we grow is changing

Canola and lentils have climbed the ranks since 1990. Canola went from the 12th biggest grain crop in 1989–90 to the 3rd in the 2026–27 forecast. Lentils were barely grown in 1989–90 and are now 4th. Wheat and barley have stayed first and second the whole time.

Over the same years, oats and lupins have come to be sown on less land than in 1989–90.
